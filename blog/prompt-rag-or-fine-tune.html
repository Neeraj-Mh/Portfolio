Prompt, RAG or fine-tune? A decision framework for LLM products

Neeraj Maheshwari · 28 October 2026 · 7 min read

Every team building on large language models eventually hits the same fork in the road: do we write a better prompt, wire up retrieval, or train the model? Picking the wrong branch burns weeks and budget. Here is the simple rule I use: start with the cheapest tool and escalate only when you have evidence it isn’t enough.

Think of it as a ladder, not a menu

Prompting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and fine-tuning are often presented as three equal options. They aren’t. They sit on a ladder of rising cost, effort and maintenance burden. Each rung is worth climbing only after the one below has demonstrably failed against your quality bar.

ApproachWhat it fixesCost & effortTime to first result
Prompt engineeringUnclear instructions, tone, output formatLowestHours
RAGMissing, private or fresh knowledgeMedium: pipeline, index, retrieval qualityDays to weeks
Fine-tuningMissing skill, style or consistent behaviourHighest: data curation, training, evalsWeeks

Rung 1: Prompt engineering

Always start here

Before writing any retrieval code or labelling any training data, find out how far clear instructions can take you. Modern models already carry a lot of general capability; often they just need to be told exactly what you want. Prompting is the right tool for:

  • Persona and tone: “Respond as a mortgage operations analyst writing for a credit manager.”
  • Strict output formats: “Return only valid JSON matching this schema.”
  • Self-contained tasks: summarising, translating or classifying text that is already in the prompt.

The question to ask: could a smart generalist do this well if I briefed them properly? If yes, invest in the brief. Few-shot examples, where you show the model two or three worked input–output pairs, are surprisingly powerful and frequently close the gap on their own.

Move up a rung only when you have evidence, not a hunch. That evidence usually looks like one of two things:

  • Stubborn unreliability: even with good examples, a meaningful share of outputs still break the format or ignore instructions.
  • A knowledge wall: the model plainly says, or confidently invents, things it cannot know, such as your internal policies or last quarter’s numbers.

Rung 2: Retrieval-augmented generation

When the model doesn’t know enough

If the model is capable of the task but lacks the facts, give it the facts at query time. RAG retrieves relevant passages from your own sources and places them in the context window alongside the question. It shines for:

  • Private or proprietary content: policy manuals, product documents, customer-specific records.
  • Information that changes: rates, procedures, news, anything newer than the model’s training cut-off.
  • Grounding and traceability: answers that cite their sources, which matters enormously in regulated industries.

The question to ask: is the failure caused by the model not knowing something? If so, RAG is almost always the right first move. It is cheaper than training, you can update knowledge by re-indexing a document instead of retraining a model, and it reduces hallucination by anchoring answers in real text.

From practice: in banking, an assistant that answers credit-policy questions is a textbook RAG use case. Policies change, auditors want to see where an answer came from, and you never want a model “remembering” a superseded rule baked into its weights.

RAG has a ceiling too. You know you have hit it when retrieval is clearly working, meaning the right context is in front of the model, yet the output is still wrong. Imagine feeding it the exact clause from a complex legal agreement and getting back a summary that is too casual and misses the nuance a specialist would catch. That is no longer a knowledge problem. It is a skill problem.

Rung 3: Fine-tuning

When the model can’t do it well enough

Fine-tuning continues training a pre-trained model on a curated, task-specific dataset, adjusting its parameters so it behaves the way you need. It is the most powerful rung and the most expensive one, so reach for it only after prompting and RAG have been genuinely exhausted. It earns its place when you need:

  • A complex, consistent skill: a distinctive brand voice, a domain’s specialised vocabulary, or a multi-step reasoning pattern the model doesn’t follow reliably.
  • Near-perfect reliability on a narrow task: extraction or classification that has to be right almost every single time.
  • Efficiency: teaching a smaller, cheaper model to match a larger one on one specific job, cutting latency and serving costs.

The question to ask: does the model fail even when it has all the right information? If yes, you are changing behaviour, not adding knowledge, and that is what fine-tuning is for. A common mistake is to fine-tune in order to teach facts. It is costly, the facts go stale, and RAG does that job better.

The good news is that fine-tuning is far more accessible than it used to be. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA (low-rank adaptation) train a small set of added weights, often under 1% of the model’s total, instead of retraining everything. That brings cost and hardware needs down to a level most teams can afford.

The decision flow on one page

What this means for product managers

  • Define the quality bar first. “Good enough” has to be measurable, with an eval set and a target, or you can’t tell when a rung has failed.
  • Diagnose before you build. Separate “doesn’t know” from “can’t do”. They lead to completely different solutions and budgets.
  • The rungs combine. Production systems often use all three: a fine-tuned model, fed by RAG, steered by a carefully engineered prompt.
  • Factor in maintenance, not just build cost. A prompt is edited in minutes and an index is refreshed in hours, but a fine-tuned model needs retraining, re-evaluation and governance every time things change.

The takeaway

Treat LLM customisation as an escalation path: prompt first, retrieve when knowledge is missing, and fine-tune only when behaviour is. Teams that follow this order ship sooner, spend less, and end up with systems that are far easier to maintain and explain to risk, compliance and the business.
